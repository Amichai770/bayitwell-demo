# Can a Kohen enter a cemetery?

By Rabbi Amichai Cohen · Mishmeret · Draft for review, September 28, 2026

**The short answer.** No. A Kohen does not enter a cemetery, does not stand within four amot (about two meters) of a grave, and does not come under the same roof as a dead body, unless the person who died is one of his closest relatives. The rule comes straight from the Torah, and it is one of the few mitzvot that shape a Kohen's life every single week. Below is what the sources actually say, where the real questions are, and what I think the rule is teaching us.

## Where the rule comes from

The Torah says it to Aharon's sons directly.

וַיֹּאמֶר יְהוָה אֶל־מֹשֶׁה אֱמֹר אֶל־הַכֹּהֲנִים בְּנֵי אַהֲרֹן וְאָמַרְתָּ אֲלֵהֶם לְנֶפֶשׁ לֹא־יִטַּמָּא בְּעַמָּיו

"Speak to the Kohanim, the sons of Aharon, and say to them: none shall become impure for a dead person among his people." Vayikra 21:1

The next verses list the exceptions. A Kohen does become impure, and in fact is obligated to, for his mother, his father, his son, his daughter, his brother, and his sister who was never married. The Sages add his wife. The Rambam sets this out in Hilchot Avel, chapter two. For those seven, a Kohen goes to the funeral, stands at the grave, and mourns like anyone else. For everyone else in the world, he keeps his distance.

## What "impure" means in practice

Tumat met, the impurity of the dead, passes in three ways: by touching, by carrying, and by being under one roof with the body. The Rambam opens Hilchot Tumat Met with exactly this. The third one, ohel, is the one that matters for a cemetery, a funeral home, and a hospital, because a Kohen can become impure without touching anything at all.

So the practical rules are these. A Kohen keeps four amot from a grave. If there is a proper wall or fence at least ten tefachim high between him and the grave, he may stand right up against it, which is why many cemeteries have a Kohanim area outside the fence. He does not enter a building that holds a body. This is codified in the Shulchan Aruch, Yoreh De'ah 371.

## The questions people actually ask

**A close friend died. May I go?** This is the hardest one, and the answer is still no. The Torah's exceptions are relatives, not relationships. What a Kohen can do is stand outside the fence, come to the shiva house, and carry the family in every other way. Every Kohen knows the feeling of standing at the gate while everyone else walks in. The Torah is not asking him to feel nothing. It is asking him to hold the boundary while feeling everything.

**What about the grave of a tzaddik?** There is a real difference of opinion. Some rely on the teaching that the righteous do not transmit impurity, and there are Kohanim who visit certain kevarim on that basis. Most poskim do not permit it. This is a question for your rav, not for an article.

**Non-Jewish cemeteries?** The Rambam holds that the graves of non-Jews do not transmit impurity through ohel, only by touch and carrying (Hilchot Tumat Met 1:13). The Shulchan Aruch (Yoreh De'ah 372:2) says a Kohen should be careful anyway. In practice, Kohanim are stringent.

**Hospitals?** A hospital may hold a body somewhere in the building, and whether that makes the whole building one ohel depends on doors, corridors, and construction. There are lenient and strict opinions, and hospitals in Israel often have arrangements for Kohanim. Ask your rav before a visit that is not an emergency, and go without asking if it is one. Life comes first.

**Flying over a cemetery, or driving past one?** Driving past on an open road is fine. Flying over is discussed by contemporary poskim and the common practice is lenient. Again, ask.

## What the rule is teaching

Why would the Torah build a fence between the Kohen and death?

The Kohen is the one who stands at the altar, and the altar is entirely on the side of life. Blood on the altar is life given, not life lost. The whole avodah is about bringing a person closer to G-d while he is alive. So the Kohen, who carries that avodah in his body even when there is no Mikdash, is kept on the side of life. Not because death is bad. Because his job is the other direction.

I think there is something in this for every Kohen who has stood outside the fence. The distance is not an exclusion. It is a statement about what you are for. Aharon's sons were told this at the very beginning, before they had done anything, and the Torah says "among his people," be'amav. You are still among your people when you stand at the gate. You are simply standing where a Kohen stands.

## A reflection to carry with you

The next time you are at the edge of a cemetery, notice the boundary instead of resenting it. Ask what it means that your priesthood is kept on the side of the living, and what you are going to do with that today.
